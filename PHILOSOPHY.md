# How I think about growth

## Systems before campaigns

Most growth teams optimize individual channels. A well-run paid social program, a solid email sequence, a retention playbook. The compounding advantage comes from building the infrastructure that connects them: shared experiment memory, cross-channel attribution, a hypothesis format that makes learnings portable across brands and quarters.

Growth OS is the concrete example of this. It exists because most growth teams I have worked with or alongside track their experiments in spreadsheets that do not talk to each other and have no institutional memory. A test that failed at one brand for a specific reason gets re-run at another brand six months later because the learning was never captured in a retrievable form. The tool forces documentation at every stage: hypothesis, kill criteria, incrementality note, learning. The value is not the dashboard. It is the discipline the structure creates.

The campaign calendar is the visible work. The system underneath it is where the compounding happens.

## Experimentation as a velocity game

The goal is not to run better experiments. It is to run more cycles in less time so learnings accumulate faster. That means compressing the loop: roadmap, hypothesis, parameter-setting, results-reading, decision. Each stage has friction points where most teams slow down or skip steps entirely.

The skip that costs the most is kill criteria. Teams define what success looks like but rarely define what failure looks like before the test runs. The result is experiments that stay alive past the point where they should be called, because no one wants to be the one to call them. Growth OS makes kill criteria a required field. You cannot move a test to Running without documenting the condition that ends it. That one constraint changes how people write hypotheses in the first place.

Statistical validity is the other common failure. Sample size is either ignored or eyeballed. The Test Validity Panel in Growth OS builds a two-proportion z-test directly into every initiative. It calculates sessions needed per variant at 90 or 95 percent confidence before a test runs, and shows live z-statistics and confidence levels as results come in. The goal is not to add bureaucracy. It is to remove the excuse for calling a winner too early.

## AI as cognitive infrastructure

The clearest way I have found to describe how I use AI: it handles the repetitive cognitive work, I handle the judgment calls.

In Growth OS, that means AI drafts hypothesis structure from a rough idea, suggests ICE scores with written rationale, and synthesizes learning patterns across closed initiatives. None of those outputs replace a decision. They remove the blank-page friction that slows down the step before the decision.

In deepstint, the constraint was different. The tool needed to stay out of the way entirely. One text field, one button, you are working. The AI layer there is minimal by design, a reflection layer at session end rather than an active participant during the session.

The design principle is the same in both: identify where cognitive friction slows the actual work, then build the automation that removes it. That is a different question than "where can I add an AI feature." Most places where AI gets added, it is an answer to the second question. The first question is harder and produces better tools.
